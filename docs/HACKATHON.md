## 2. Judging rubric and the Finance prompt (verbatim from [Packet])

### Judging Criteria

- **Problem & Potential Impact — 25%**
  Does the project address a meaningful problem within the selected track? Is the intended user
  clearly defined, and does the team explain how the solution could create a useful, positive impact?

- **Technical Execution & Completeness — 25%**
  How well does the prototype deliver its core functionality? Are the technical choices appropriate
  for the problem, and can the team explain how the solution works? Consider the quality of
  implementation and what the team accomplished during the hackathon.

- **User Experience & Design — 25%**
  Is the solution intuitive and useful for its intended audience? Can users complete the core
  workflow with minimal friction? Consider the clarity of the interface, thoughtful design choices,
  and attention to accessibility.

- **Innovation & Creativity — 15%**
  Does the project introduce a fresh idea, apply technology in a clever way, or meaningfully improve
  an existing solution? Consider what makes the approach distinctive and how that originality adds
  value.

- **Demo & Presentation — 10%**
  Does the team clearly communicate the problem, demonstrate the solution, and explain its value? Can
  they answer questions about their approach and clearly distinguish working functionality from
  simulated features and future plans?

### Finance: Make Money Management More Accessible

Build tools that help individuals, small businesses, or communities understand their finances, make
informed decisions, or complete financial tasks more easily.

Challenges to explore:

- Help people with irregular income budget for bills and unexpected expenses.
- Make financial products, fees, and tradeoffs easier to understand and compare.
- Help users recognize suspicious transactions or potential scams.
- Simplify tasks such as tracking expenses, managing invoices, or sending payments.

**Your goal:** Demonstrate how your solution improves a specific financial decision or workflow for a
clearly defined user.

### What the rubric implies for us

- 75% of the score is split evenly across problem/user, technical execution and UX. Name one user
  (Jordan) and one decision in the first 20 seconds; the Finance goal asks for exactly that.
- "What the team accomplished during the hackathon" is scored, and the repo "should reflect work
  completed during the hackathon". Our prep doc planned a pre-made starter template "made before the
  event". If any of that is in this repo, say so in the README and the submission. The FAQ also says
  not to start hacking before Saturday noon.
- The 10% demo criterion explicitly scores whether we "distinguish working functionality from
  simulated features and future plans". Put a real / simulated / next slide in the deck: Plaid
  sandbox and synthetic transactions are simulated, and the card terms and engine are real.
- "Attention to accessibility" is part of UX: contrast, keyboard focus, phone width.
- The preliminary Q&A is 1 minute, so the 4 minutes must cover all six listed points without help
  from questions.

## 4. Judges

The Partiful page is the only official source for the judges. Background below is from public pages,
and how far it applies to each judge is noted.

**Kaushal Patel, Member of Technical Staff, OpenAI.** The likely match is a LinkedIn profile
(linkedin.com/in/kaushal21) headlined "Applied AI @ OpenAI": Northwestern, a move to Member of
Technical Staff at OpenAI after Meta and Google (self-reported, and not confirmed to be the same
person). **What to emphasise:** where the model stops and deterministic code starts. Ace's line, "the AI
never does the math", is the right story for him. Show that the dollar figures come from the rules
engine and optimizer, with the LLM limited to cleaning merchant names and handling category
fallback. Expect questions about hallucinated numbers, how we evaluated the model's part, and
latency. Don't pitch anything that looks like "chat with your money", since ChatGPT already ships
finance features. Lead with what a general assistant can't do: cap-aware optimization over a real
year of purchases, and the click-through to sourced, dated card terms.

**Franck Leveneur, founder and CEO, Data-Sleek; ex-Hulu (per Partiful).** Data-Sleek's own site calls
him a data architect with 30+ years of experience and a former UCLA Anderson MSBA faculty member. The
firm does data strategy, warehousing (Snowflake, Microsoft Fabric), dbt/Fivetran pipelines,
SingleStore/MySQL, governance (PII, GDPR, SOX) and "AI readiness". The Hulu role appears only on
Partiful. **What to emphasise:** the data layer. Cover the schema (Txn, Card, rules), how Plaid's
categories map to reward categories and where that mapping is wrong (merchant category codes, Costco
and Walmart exclusions), how the terms for ~25-29 cards were sourced and dated and how they would
stay fresh, and data quality checks. Mention PII: no card numbers, ever, and a sandbox or synthetic
year only. He'll respect a clean, auditable data model more than animation.

**Saty Raghavachary, Associate Professor of Computer Science Practice, USC Viterbi.** His faculty page
lists teaching in databases, search engines, data science, computer graphics and intro programming.
He spent 16 years at DreamWorks Feature Animation building CG tools, and he researches dataflow
computation and AGI. **What to emphasise:** explain how it works the way you'd teach it. One clear
diagram (transactions → category map → rules → optimizer → receipt), and why caps make the
assignment an LP / min-cost-flow problem rather than greedy, with the number that shows LP beating
greedy on Jordan's year. Show the hand-computed test cases. He'll also notice the visual quality of
the Replay and Sankey views, so make them correct as well as pretty.

**Chris Lin, founder of Cylier; ex-Uber senior staff engineer (per Partiful).** A LinkedIn profile
(Arcadia, CA) lists him as Cylier's founder. Cylier is a real-estate investment tool that scans
thousands of listings a day, computes ROI, cash flow and cap rate, and sends investors pre-analyzed
deals. So he has built a consumer product that turns messy listings into trustworthy financial
numbers. **What to emphasise:** whether the numbers hold up (interest computed from average daily
balance × APR, fees, credits), product sense (who pays, and why that's us and not a card-referral
site), and how it would scale to real Plaid data and many users. He's likely to test the
assumptions behind the net figure, so be ready to unfold any number on screen to its rule and its
transactions.
